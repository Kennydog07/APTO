A letter from Father Christmas is one of those small things children remember for years. The best ones feel **personal**: they mention real things your child did this year, in a voice that is warm, a little magical and completely British.

## What to include

- **Your child's first name**, early on, so the letter feels written just for them.
- **One to three specific good things** from this year: learning to swim, being brave at the dentist, helping with the shopping. Specific beats general – "you helped Grandma plant bulbs" is far more magical than "you've been good".
- **A gentle encouragement**, phrased as something Father Christmas believes they can do ("keep being kind to your little brother").
- **Little North Pole details**: the reindeer, the elves, mince pies, the sleigh being polished for Christmas Eve.
- **A brother, sister or pet by name**, if you like. Children love it when Father Christmas knows the dog.
- **A short P.S.** – Rudolph saying thank you for the carrot always goes down well.

## What to leave out

- **Promises of a specific present.** Plans change and shops sell out. Say the elves have read their wish list, not that it is on the way.
- **Threats or guilt.** "Santa is watching", coal and "be good or else" can genuinely worry young children. Keep it warm.
- **Money and other children.** No prices, and no comparisons with friends or siblings.

## Getting the length right

For ages 2–4, keep it short and read it aloud. For 5–10, aim for about 150–300 words: long enough to feel special, short enough to read in one sitting.

## Making it look like it came from the North Pole

Print it on cream paper if you have some, add a festive border, sign it "Father Christmas", and pop it in an envelope addressed to your child "c/o the fireplace". A certificate saying they're on the Nice List is a lovely extra.
